Epicure Robotics builds machines that make drinks — smoothies, tender coconut, blended beverages — and puts them where people are, with nobody standing behind the counter. I joined in November 2024 as one of two engineers, and I've been on it since, alongside college.

## Context

A drink-making robot is a strange product: it's a shop with no shopkeeper. It has to greet the customer, take their money, make the drink, clean itself, and tell someone when something goes wrong — at 2 a.m., with no one around. Every part of that is software.

## What I built

- **The screen customers use** — choosing a drink, paying, watching it get made. I care about this as much as anything under it: if the screen confuses someone, nothing behind it matters.
- **The fleet behind it** — orders, payments, stock, and the dashboard the operations team runs the business from, across three product lines.
- **The link to the machine** — how the cloud tells each robot to dispense, move, lock and clean, and hears back when something goes wrong.
- **Kiosks that look after themselves** — they start on their own, recover from crashes, and can be fixed remotely instead of with a trip across town.
- **Partners** — a way for SmartQ, an ordering app with 1M+ installs, to send live orders straight to our machines.
- **Supply chain** — stock tracked from the warehouse to the machine.

<CaseCallout label="the lesson" claim="Unattended is a different standard.">
When a machine runs with nobody watching, every bug costs a drink, a payment, or a customer who doesn't come back. It taught me to plan for the boring failure — the dropped Wi-Fi, the stuck door — as the main case, and to treat the customer's thirty seconds at the screen as the whole product.
</CaseCallout>

## Why it matters to what's next

Two years with these machines showed me where intelligence is missing: machines that can't see what they're doing, can't explain a fault, and can't ask the cloud because the network isn't there. That's the gap I want to build into.
